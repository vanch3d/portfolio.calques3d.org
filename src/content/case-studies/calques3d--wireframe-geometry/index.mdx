# Calques 3D: letting teachers decide how students see a cube

Secondary students could not read a 3D figure drawn on a 2D screen, so I built a wireframe geometry microworld with the teachers who would use it, and their pedagogy shaped its model.

## A cube on a flat screen

French secondary students are taught spatial geometry from drawings, and a drawing of a cube does not tell you what is in front of what. Students were often unable to see the figure behind the drawing, and so could not reason about it. Physical models help until the property you want to see is inside a solid object. The microworld was for ordinary classes (no preparation for the student) and had to let a teacher decide which construction tools a given activity offered.

## A deliberately plain rendering, and a reference frame that teachers chose

Calques 3D draws wireframe only, and that was a decision, not a limit. Wireframe is what secondary education works with, and the software followed it. Early on I added a realistic 3D rendering myself, as the engineer and designer, because OpenGL was the thing of the moment, but the teachers took it out. In the paper-and-pencil approach, the difficulty of reading a wireframe in an orthogonal system is precisely the pedagogical aim. Realistic rendering also gives volume to objects that have none: lines become tubes, points balls and planes thin boxes, all against their mathematical definition. Pedagogy (didactics, as the French call it) decided what the screen showed.

![The same file with OpenGL shading](images/27-20140421113729-6ec5bdca.png 'The same construction in a realistic rendering: lines become tubes and points balls, which the mathematics does not allow.')

A wireframe lets you see through a solid, so a property that sits inside a cube (a plane section, say) stays visible. The cost is that everything is translucent, and relative position becomes hard to read. I did not answer that with realism. I added a configurable spatial frame, a horizontal "floor" and "dividing walls", onto which points and intersections project, with the parts behind a wall or under the floor drawn differently.

The rest of the interaction follows the same reasoning. Figures are built step by step, with feedback on what is being created. A figure is deformed only by dragging its movable points ("the geometry of the point", because we could not offer direct manipulation of arbitrary points of a 3D object), and the whole figure is recomputed live. A "tracing" extracts part of a construction into its own synchronised view, so a triangle can be seen face-on, real-sized, while the cube stays in the main view.

## What the teachers changed

We worked with two groups of teachers, one from general education and one from a technical lycée, in working sessions, with the software as the thing on the table. At the first presentation of the prototype, the author-teachers rejected the orthonormal frame I had put in, which is what the literature and similar tools used. Their reasons were that it belongs to analytic geometry, which we had left out, and that in their experience it gave too weak a base for reading objects in space. The floor, the walls and the other options came out of those discussions and the successive tests, and none of them is compulsory: the prescribing teacher can fix the frame for an activity, or leave it to the student.

The same sessions settled what counts as a basic object. Computing practice said to build a tetrahedron from points and segments. The teachers pointed out that "let ABCD be a tetrahedron" does not carry the same faces. The compromise was the cube, the sphere, the cylinder and the cone as basic objects, and reusable constructions later.

Tracings were the 3D extension of the principle of the original Calques (2D), so teachers asked for them from the start. What took the back and forth was making them work in a 2.5D interface, and we settled it in group-based incremental prototyping. Dragging was harder. A proposal for dragging in 3D with a 2D mouse and a keyboard was debated hotly and tested, with no winner, which taught me that people get used to whatever novel interface they are given.

## How I work

I put a running prototype in front of the people who will use it, and let their expertise decide what the model contains before I decide what it looks like.
