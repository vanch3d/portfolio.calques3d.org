# Auditory games: turning a listening exercise into something tinnitus patients choose to play

Tinnitus patients were asked to repeat a dull listening task for thirty minutes a day, so I built a playable game around that task and let patients who had done it before test it with me until it made sense to them.

## A dull task, done at home, five times a week

The players were adults with chronic tinnitus, recruited through ear, nose and throat and audiology departments in Nottingham. The training task is frequency discrimination: you hear tones and say which one is different, and the task gets harder as the difference between the tones shrinks. It is effective only if it is repeated, and the conventional software that delivers it (a nature backdrop, a sound cue for right or wrong, a single level) is repetitive, so adherence was the concern. In the trial, each participant trained at home on a loaned laptop for thirty minutes, five times a week, over six weeks.

## A game whose mechanic is the task

I designed and built one of the two games, Treasure Hunter, as a working prototype from the start. The player steers a mining cart left and right over buried nuggets, hears three tones, and decides whether the second one is higher than the standard, which means gold, or not, which means coal. I made the listening decision the thing the player does, rather than a quiz placed between rewards, because, from learning and game theory, a reward that comes from the task itself (intrinsic integration) should keep people at it longer than points attached to it. Gold buried deeper is worth more, and difficulty rises by shrinking the frequency gap that signals gold. Feedback and scores appear after each level, and each session restarts from the level reached the last time, so a patient never repeats ground only to warm up.

## What patients changed before the trial

Before the main trial, five people who had already done the conventional training took part in beta testing of both games. I watched them play in realistic training conditions and then interviewed each of them, semi-structured, about what they liked and disliked. We repeated this in rounds, checking that the game responded consistently to what the player did, that the game status and the instructions were clear, and that anything disliked was removed.
In the trial, we collected a short usability questionnaire for each game and a ranking at the end. I designed, ran and analysed the usability and desirability tests, interviewing participants with product reaction cards to start the conversation. In the interviews, the themes that make a game intrinsically motivating (challenge, control, fantasy, curiosity) came out mostly positive for Treasure Hunter, where for the conventional software they were as often negative as positive.

## How I work

I put something playable in front of the people who will use it, and I let what they say and do with it correct me. I also decide first how the work will be judged (here, training separated from testing, and engagement measured separately from outcome).
