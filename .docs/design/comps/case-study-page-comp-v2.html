Calques 3D: letting teachers decide how students see a cube

Secondary students could not read a 3D figure drawn on a 2D screen, so I built a wireframe geometry microworld with the teachers who would use it, and their pedagogy shaped its model.

  • Participatory design
  • Interaction design
  • Direct manipulation
  • Prototyping

1A cube on a flat screen

French secondary students are taught spatial geometry from drawings, and a drawing of a cube does not tell you what is in front of what. Students were often unable to see the figure behind the drawing, and so could not reason about it. Physical models help until the property you want to see is inside a solid object. The microworld was for ordinary classes (no preparation for the student) and had to let a teacher decide which construction tools a given activity offered.

2A deliberately plain rendering, and a reference frame that teachers chose

Calques 3D draws wireframe only, and that was a decision, not a limit. Wireframe is what secondary education works with, and the software followed it. Early on I added a realistic 3D rendering myself, as the engineer and designer, because OpenGL was the thing of the moment. The teachers took it out. In the paper-and-pencil approach, the difficulty of reading a wireframe in an orthogonal system is precisely the pedagogical aim. Realistic rendering also gives volume to objects that have none: lines become tubes, points balls and planes thin boxes, all against their mathematical definition. Pedagogy (didactics, as the French call it) decides, not computer imagery.

A wireframe lets you see through a solid, so a property that sits inside a cube (a plane section, say) stays visible. The cost is that everything is translucent, and relative position becomes hard to read. I did not answer that with realism. I added a configurable spatial frame, a horizontal "floor" and "dividing walls", onto which points and intersections project, with the parts behind a wall or under the floor drawn differently.

The rest of the interaction follows the same reasoning. Figures are built step by step, with feedback on what is being created. A figure is deformed only by dragging its movable points ("the geometry of the point", because we could not offer direct manipulation of arbitrary points of a 3D object), and the whole figure is recomputed live. A "tracing" extracts part of a construction into its own synchronised view, so a triangle can be seen face-on, real-sized, while the cube stays in the main view.

3What the teachers changed

We worked with two groups of teachers, one from general education and one from a technical lycée, in working sessions, with the software as the thing on the table. At the first presentation of the prototype, the author-teachers rejected the orthonormal frame I had put in, which is what the literature and similar tools used. Their reasons were that it belongs to analytic geometry, which we had left out, and that in their experience it gave too weak a base for reading objects in space. The floor, the walls and the other options came out of those discussions and the successive tests, and none of them is compulsory: the prescribing teacher can fix the frame for an activity, or leave it to the student.

The same sessions settled what counts as a basic object. Computing practice said to build a tetrahedron from points and segments; the teachers pointed out that "let ABCD be a tetrahedron" does not carry the same faces. The compromise was the cube, the sphere, the cylinder and the cone as basic objects, and reusable constructions later.

Tracings were the 3D extension of the principle of the original Calques (2D), so teachers asked for them from the start. What took the back and forth was making them work in a 2.5D interface, and we settled it in group-based incremental prototyping. Dragging was harder. A proposal for dragging in 3D with a 2D mouse and a keyboard was debated hotly and tested, with no winner, which taught me that people get used to whatever novel interface they are given.

4How I work

I put a running prototype in front of the people who will use it, and let their expertise decide what the model contains before I decide what it looks like.

HiveMQ Edge: A Design Retrospective

Over three years at HiveMQ, I made the same design bet twice: that a graph-canvas paradigm was the right way to configure an industrial IoT gateway. I got two different outcomes. This is the record of both, what held up, what didn't, why, and what I actually do differently now, including how I work with AI agents on real production problems, not just how I'd describe it in an interview.

Closing

None of this started at HiveMQ. At Matillion, I worked on the visual ETL job designer, another domain where the underlying structure, data flowing through a sequence of transformation steps, is genuinely graph-shaped, and where a canvas-based interface matches the domain rather than imposing a metaphor on it. Workspace was a second, independent bet on the same underlying design principle, in a much higher-stakes, backend and IoT-first engineering culture than a data-tooling company, where a canvas-first paradigm was less of a known quantity going in. DataHub complicated that bet. The domain ontology work underneath it showed its real limits. The ownership work shows where the practice has actually gone since, not less rigorous, differently structured.

The Same Bet, a Harder Domain: DataHub

The same paradigm that worked for Workspace was rebuilt from scratch for DataHub, five months later, same person, same underlying technology. The reason wasn't that the domain was wrong for a canvas, the message-transformation pipeline genuinely has graph-shaped structure. It was that "OT technician" and "data engineer" were treated as job titles rather than researched as personas, and a canvas paradigm has to earn its fit with a specific, well-understood audience, not just a logically coherent domain.

I built DataHub's initial flow architecture and visual language in PRs #262 to #271, January 2024, and continued extending it through early 2026, on React Flow, Zustand (a dual store for draft and validation state), RJSF, Monaco Editor, and AJV8.

3.1The problem

DataHub lets users define two kinds of MQTT policy: data policies, which transform or validate messages, and behaviour policies, which define client state machines. Both are, structurally, pipelines: something goes in, passes through conditions and transformations, something happens at the end. Having just shipped Workspace, the design bet was to reach for the same paradigm again: a React Flow canvas, drag-and-drop nodes, wire them together, publish.

3.2What it grew into

Like Workspace, DataHub kept receiving real investment for two years, not an abandoned experiment:

  • Dry-run validation — policies are checked against a validation endpoint before publish, so every error surfaces in one pass rather than being discovered one round-trip at a time.
  • Resource management — JSON and Protobuf schemas, and JavaScript transformation functions, as reusable, versioned resources rather than inline blobs.

3.3The honest part

The repository's own architecture documentation carries a maintainer warning, current as of this writing:

"The Data Hub UI has been redesigned from scratch for integration within HiveMQ Cloud Connect V2 (CCV2)... the DataHub Designer described in this document is no longer actively developed and should be treated as legacy code."

The metaphor strained under assumed complexity, complexity the interface was built to handle whether or not the real audience needed it handled that way. The deeper issue sat upstream, in the research. Industrial environments run on decades-old equipment, which is exactly why HiveMQ Edge needs adapters for legacy protocols in the first place.
