Design by building

I design and build the interface as one practice, in two eras with one continuous proof. These case studies are about that way of working: I design the interface by building it. I start from a working prototype, not from wireframes, put it in front of the people who will use it, and let what they do with it decide what changes next. Where a design has to be shown before anything works, I lay out live but non-functional pages with the same components, and take the screenshots from there. The prototype is an early version of the real feature, so it grows into the product instead of being thrown away.

The method comes from twenty years of research and development, where participatory design was the norm, and from industry, where I applied it to shipped products. In each case I try to stand in the place of the person using the thing, and I trust a real user's first five minutes over my own expectations.

HiveMQ Edge is the production case: an industrial IoT product whose interface I carried from interaction design to release, as the only frontend engineer. Three short studies cover a data-mapping editor that shows its working, one theme as the single source of styling for every component, and a codeless policy designer that checks before it publishes. All the screens come from the public repository.

The earlier cases come from research, where the users were students, teachers, patients and mature learners: OpenEssayist (feedback on a draft essay), Calques 3D (why a wireframe, not a realistic cube), MyPlan (a learner's history on a timeline) and the tinnitus games (a listening task made playable).

Each case is short: the problem and the people, what I built, what changed once people used it, and how I work.
