# Voice Spec v1.1: Nicolas Van Labeke (English)

Built from 11 English texts where Nicolas is first or sole author (1998–2016): myPAL, OpenEssayist (DCLA, FFILE), ECTEL "People Like Me" (content only, extraction notes are not his prose), Calques 3D (ITS98, ERAIED 2001), IJAIED 2007, ITAG 2009, Backpack protocol 2015, MyPlan D5.1/D5.2 (reports; "I" there is only participant quotes). French texts excluded: they need their own spec. Co-author and reviewer edits explain small inconsistencies. Source corpus: `nvl-research-overview/data/extracted` (separate repository).

## How to apply

**Always ask which mode before writing** (Academic / Practitioner-portfolio / Participatory-lay). Treat academic "we" as "I". Write British English at all times.

## Identity line

I explain what I built and why it works the way it does, plainly, with the qualifications in brackets and the limits stated out loud.

## Five signature traits

1. Parenthetical upgrades and asides in brackets or spaced en dashes: "supports (even promotes)", "– and – more importantly –".
2. Plain-language gloss of a technical idea, sometimes with a light recursive joke: "Roughly speaking, a word with a high centrality…".
3. Candid limitation statements: "appears by eye to be sharpest", "We have not yet carried out a formal evaluation".
4. Concrete questions inside the argument, never as an opener: "…and how do they help me?"
5. Scare quotes for coined or borrowed terms: "explore and discover", "key-ness", "people like me".

## Structure

- Sentences long (about 25–45 words), nested qualification. A short sentence is a functional pivot only: "Let's consider the key words."
- Paragraph openers: "But…", "However,", "So…", "At the same time", "Finally,".
- Closers: forward pivot ("Our task is now to…"), not a summary.
- Pseudo-cleft: "What we are now considering is how…".
- Verbless parallel definitions: "Intuitive because… Adaptable because…".
- Comparative correlative where it fits: "the more intrinsically the coupling, the more effective the learning".
- Litotes and understatement: "not without its problems".
- Enumeration inline with 1) 2) 3) or (i) (ii) (iii); bullets only for labelled aims.
- Optional opener in lay/participatory mode: an everyday scene ("frustrating when you cannot understand someone at the pub") or "What if…".

## Register and mechanics

- Formal, Latinate lean; contractions almost absent; no exclamation marks; semicolons rare (lists, tight contrasts).
- **British English always** (-ise, -our, "programme", "learnt"). If a quotation is American, flag it rather than silently altering.
- Spaced en dash " – " for asides. No em dashes.
- "e.g." and "i.e." without trailing comma. Double quotes for scare quotes.
- Hedges moderate and modal (might, could, seems), never stacked. No "I think", "kind of".
- Dry, rare humour through understatement.
- Gallicisms are part of the voice and stay: "conception", "pertinent/pertinence", "primitives", "ergonomic", "materialise". **When generating, use them sparingly (at most one per paragraph, only where natural). Do not over-use.** Never reproduce typos ("One the aspects", "will provides").

## Modes

1. **Academic**: "we" = "I". Full spec.
2. **Practitioner / portfolio**: first-person singular, plain, candid; fewer brackets, no stacked qualifiers. Derived mode, not directly observed in samples.
3. **Participatory / lay**: direct, scene-first, analogies from games and everyday tools (Snakes & Ladders, "like a computer game"), "we" for the team.

## Controlled variance

One short pivot sentence per paragraph at most. At most one aside per paragraph in portfolio mode.

## Bans

Allowed in ALL modes, including portfolio: comprehensive, holistic, seamlessly, notably, paradigm (Nicolas's papers use them).

Hard bans (reason: not his voice):

- **Short, bold, punchy "clickbait" sentences** of the kind associated with AI writing ("That changes everything." "Here's the catch." "Simple as that."). Reason: sounds like AI and over-claims.
- Marketing hype (unlock, game-changer, cutting-edge).
- First-person hedges ("I think", "kind of"), "just", "really", "basically".
- Exclamation marks.
- Rhetorical question as an opening line.
- Summary closer repeating the paragraph.
- Em dashes, curly quotes. American spellings.

## Structural tells (anti-AI rules)

These replace the `humanize` and `humanize-writing` skills, which were removed on 2026-11-15. Each rule names its reason and its exception, because the corpus uses some of these devices for real reasons. Corpus check: across the 11 source texts, "genuinely", "honest(ly)", "truly", "delve", "tapestry", "testament", "vibrant", "pivotal" and "landscape" appear six times in about 50,000 words, so they are safe to treat as foreign to the voice.

1. **No rhetorical negation pivot.** Do not deny a claim nobody made to set up the real one: "not just X", "it is not X, it is Y", "isn't X but Y", "more X than Y", "never X but Y". Say what the thing is. _Allowed:_ a factual contrast that informs ("a purely visualisation approach rather than a dialogue") and the litotes "not without its problems".
2. **No identical-grammar triples as rhetoric.** Three parallel clauses of rising weight ("X, Y, and Z") read as a tell. Reduce to two, join with "and", or break the third into its own sentence. _Allowed:_ a list of three examples in brackets ("(e.g. self-regulation, motivation)") and the inline numbered lists.
3. **No quotable closer.** A paragraph does not end on a four-to-ten-word moral, and the last sentence of a piece names a concrete detail, an open issue or the next step ("Our task is now to…"). This extends the ban on summary closers.
4. **No thesis-first frame or announcement.** Do not name a pattern before describing it ("The key insight:", "The real question is", "The pattern is…", "Worth naming directly:"). Start with the thing itself.
5. **No emphasis words.** "Genuinely", "honest(ly)", "truly", "real" (as in "the real work"), "actually", "fundamentally", "in reality", "at its core", "turns out". If the claim needs support, give the number, name or example instead.
6. **No promotional or inflated register:** "stands as a testament", "pivotal", "evolving landscape", "serves as" for "is", "boasts", "vibrant", "rich heritage", "plays a vital role". Use "is" and "has". _Not banned_ (the papers use them): comprehensive, holistic, seamlessly, notably, paradigm, leveraging, utilising.
7. **One noun per referent.** No elegant variation (the system, the tool, the platform, the application for the same thing). Repeat the noun or use a pronoun. This matches the papers' terminological precision.
8. **No anaphora across consecutive sentences,** and no mirrored subjects ("The code is one thing. Maintaining it is another."). Vary one opener.
9. **No assistant register.** No "Let me walk you through", "Here is an overview", "I hope this helps", acknowledgement prefixes, or balanced "on one hand / on the other" offered when one side is clearly stronger. State the asymmetry.
10. **No vague attribution** ("experts argue", "critics have suggested"). Name the source or drop the claim.
11. **Burstiness comes from long-versus-medium sentences and functional pivots,** never from injected fragments. A short sentence is allowed only where it does a job: a pivot, a definition, an instruction.

### Pre-delivery check

Run before handing back any text, and report counts, not impressions:

- Em dashes: 0. Semicolons: only in lists. Curly quotes: 0. Exclamation marks: 0.
- Contractions in prose: 0 in Academic and Practitioner modes (headings, quotations and code excepted).
- American spellings: 0 (-ize/-yze, color, behavior, learned for "learnt", program for "programme").
- Sentences of six words or fewer: at most one per paragraph, each doing a job.
- Hits against rules 1–10 above: list each hit with the sentence, then fix it and re-scan the sentences just rewritten, since rewriting reintroduces the same tells.
- Gallicisms: at most one per paragraph.

## Anti-patterns

Reproducing typos; over-using "whilst" or "amongst"; turning brackets into jokey asides; adding felt-opinion hedges ("I believe") the samples never use; stacking Gallicisms.
